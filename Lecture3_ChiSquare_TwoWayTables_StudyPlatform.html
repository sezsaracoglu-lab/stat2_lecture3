Chi-Square and Two-Way Tables

Counting people in categories — and deciding whether the pattern in the table is real.

A note before you start, Sezen

This lecture is about the most ordinary data in the world: counts of people in categories. Did they attend or not; did they pass or not. Which school type; how much they worry. There is no mean, no standard deviation, nothing to average — every variable here is qualitative, and the only raw material is a table of frequencies.

The whole lecture is one idea, repeated three times. Write down what the table would look like if there were no relationship at all — those are the expected counts. Then measure how far the real table is from that, cell by cell, and add the pieces up. That total is χ². Everything else — degrees of freedom, the critical value, the p-value — exists only to answer "is that total bigger than chance would produce?"

You will meet this constantly in HR data, because almost everything in a people dataset is categorical: promoted or not by gender, attrition by location, interview outcome by source of hire. The first slide of your own analysis will always be a cross-table — and the discipline this lecture teaches is that a difference in percentages is not a finding until you have asked what the table would have looked like by chance alone.

How to use this platform. Work through the Lessons tab in order; each lesson is one diagram, five bullets and a check. 🎯 Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture you should be able to do each of these without looking anything up.

  • ①
    Recognise when the data call for a chi-square test at all — both variables qualitative — and choose between the test of independence and the goodness-of-fit test.
  • ②
    Read a two-way table: the joint distribution, the marginal distributions and the conditional distributions, and know which one answers a question about a relationship.
  • ③
    Graph conditional distributions with a side-by-side or segmented bar graph, and read a relationship off the picture.
  • ④
    State H₀ and Ha in both the standard forms — "no relation between the variables" and "the percentages are the same in the different groups".
  • ⑤
    Compute expected counts from row total × column total ÷ table total, and explain why that formula is H₀.
  • ⑥
    Compute χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected, and say which cells contributed most.
  • ⑦
    Find df — (rows − 1)(columns − 1) for a two-way table, k − 1 for goodness of fit — and use a χ² table to reach a decision.
  • ⑧
    Check the validity conditions, report the result properly, and be honest about what it does and does not say about causality.

🧵 Synthesis

The single thesis of this lecture

Chi-square is one move, performed three times: build the table H₀ predicts, then measure how far the real table is from it. The expected counts are not a formula to memorise — they are the null hypothesis written out as a table, which is why row total × column total ÷ table total is exactly "the same percentages in every group". Each cell's (observed − expected)² / expected says how surprising that one cell is, divided by how much surprise its size can afford; χ² is the total surprise. Whether that total is large is not a question you can answer by looking at it, because a big table accumulates more of it by accident — hence degrees of freedom, which set the bar higher the more cells you have. The goodness-of-fit test changes only where the expected counts come from: a claim about proportions instead of the table's own margins. And when the test is significant, it has told you the percentages differ — not why, because a third variable outside the table can produce exactly the same counts. So: percentages to see it, expected counts to test it, degrees of freedom to judge it, and judgement to explain it.

Lesson 1 of 14 · Slides 2–4 · the flowchart

🗺️ Two flavours of chi-square

Where this test sits among all the others, and the single question that picks which version you need.

Slide 4, drawn as the decision you actually make. Both flavours need a qualitative dependent variable and a qualitative independent variable; the difference is how many variables carry the counts.

  • 🔤
    Chi-square is for frequencies. Dependent variable qualitative, independent variable qualitative — counts of people in categories, nothing to average.
  • 2️⃣
    Test for independence (also used for comparing populations): frequencies on two variables. It tells you about the relation between two nominal variables.
  • 1️⃣
    Goodness of fit: frequencies on one variable. It tells you how well the observed frequencies match your prior assumption.
  • 🧭
    On the course flowchart, chi-square is the nominal-data column: goodness of fit on the "single sample tests" row, and the test of independence on the "compare two groups, independent samples" row.
  • ⚠️
    The commonest error is upstream of the test. If either variable is really quantitative, the answer is not a better chi-square — it is a different test.
🪝 Memory hook: count the variables, not the categories. Two variables means a grid, and a grid asks "is there a relation?". One variable means a row, and a row asks "does it match what I claimed?".
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Chi-square is what you use when all you have are counts in categories. If I've got two categorical variables and want to know whether they're related, that's the test of independence. If I've got one and want to check it against a claim, that's goodness of fit."
★ Key insight

Both tests do exactly the same arithmetic — Σ (observed − expected)² / expected. They differ only in where the expected counts come from: from the table's own margins for independence, from an external claim for goodness of fit.

🎬 Independence vs goodness of fit 🎬 Choosing a test for categorical data

🧩 Comprehension check

You record each student's exam mark (0–10) and whether they attended. Chi-square?
No. The mark is quantitative, so the right test compares means — an independent-samples t test. You could force it into a chi-square by chopping marks into pass/fail, but that throws information away, and the flowchart only sends you to chi-square when the data are genuinely frequencies.
A tutor counts how many of her 240 students passed, and wants to know whether that differs between her seven tutor groups. Which test?
A test of independence — two qualitative variables (pass/fail and tutor group) crossed in a table. It is the tutor example on slide 37, with df = (2 − 1)(7 − 1) = 6.
Why does the lecture call the same test "for comparing populations" and "for independence"?
Because the arithmetic is identical and only the sampling story differs. If you drew independent samples from several populations and classified each person on one variable, you are comparing populations (homogeneity); if you drew one sample and classified everyone on two variables, you are testing independence. Slide 38 makes this explicit: the chi-square test is valid under either sampling scheme.

Lesson 2 of 14 · Slides 5–6 · the two-way table

🧱 Anatomy of a cross-table

Body, margins, grand total — three different distributions live in one table, and they answer three different questions.

Slides 5 and 15. A cross-table needs a qualitative dependent variable and a qualitative independent variable — here worrying and school type.

  • 🧱
    The body holds the joint distribution — how many people fall into each combination of the two variables.
  • ➕
    The margins hold each variable alone. Row totals describe the row variable; column totals describe the column variable.
  • 🔢
    Everything must reconcile. Row totals and column totals both sum to the grand total — the first check on any table you are given.
  • 🧮
    Size matters later. "Rows × columns" is how you will describe the table, and it is what fixes the degrees of freedom.
  • 🗣️
    Two exam questions, always: what are the variables described by this table, and how many individuals were surveyed?

★ Key insight

The margins are where the expected counts will come from later. That is the deep reason the lecture makes you read them first: H₀ says the table's inside is completely determined by its edges.

🎬 Reading a two-way table

🧩 Comprehension check

In the young-adults table, what does the number 696 describe?
A joint count: 696 individuals who are both female and answered "a 50-50 chance". It is one cell of the joint distribution — not a percentage of anything until you choose a denominator.
How many rows and columns does the worry-about-school table have, and what will its df be?
Two rows (VWO, HAVO) and three columns (no, a little, a lot) — a 2 × 3 table, so df = (2 − 1)(3 − 1) = 2.
Why can't you tell from the raw counts whether gender and opinion are related?
Because the two groups are not the same size (2 367 females, 2 459 males), so every count is partly a statement about how many people were in that group. Counts have to become percentages before any comparison is meaningful — which is exactly the next two lessons.

Lesson 3 of 14 · Slides 7–9 · marginal distributions

📊 One variable at a time

The edges of the table, turned into percentages and drawn — and the thing they cannot tell you.

Slide 9: the marginal distribution of "chance of being wealthy by age 30" for all 4 826 young adults, calculated from the row totals.

  • 📏
    The definition. The marginal distribution of one of the categorical variables in a two-way table of counts is the distribution of values of that variable among all individuals described by the table.
  • ➗
    Step 1: use the row or column totals to calculate the distribution in percents.
  • 📈
    Step 2: make a graph to display it.
  • 🎯
    Percentages beat counts, especially when the groups being compared are different sizes.
  • 🚫
    And the limitation: marginal distributions tell us nothing about the relationship between the two variables.

🪝 Memory hook: a marginal distribution is a headcount at the door. You learn how many arrived in each category — and nothing whatsoever about who came with whom.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "If I only look at the totals down the side of the table, I'm describing one variable on its own and pretending the other doesn't exist. It's useful background, but it can't show a relationship."
★ Key insight

Two datasets with identical marginal distributions can have opposite relationships inside the table. The margins are the constraints; the relationship lives in how the body is arranged within them.

🎬 Marginal distributions

🧩 Comprehension check

What is the marginal distribution of gender in the young-adults table?
2 367 / 4 826 = 49.0% female and 2 459 / 4 826 = 51.0% male. It uses the column totals, and it says nothing at all about opinions.
Why does the lecture insist that percentages are often more informative than counts?
Because a raw count confounds "how common is this response" with "how many people were in this group". With 2 367 women and 2 459 men, a larger male count can simply mean more men — the comparison only becomes fair once each group is expressed as a percentage of its own total.

Lesson 4 of 14 · Slides 10–14 · conditional distributions

🎚️ Where the relationship actually lives

Condition on one variable, compare the percentages, and draw the comparison two ways.

Slide 13: the conditional distribution of opinion among males and among females, drawn as a side-by-side bar graph. Each group's bars sum to 100% of that group.

  • 🎚️
    The definition. A conditional distribution of a variable describes the values of that variable among individuals who have a specific value of another variable.
  • 1️⃣
    Step 1: select the row(s) or column(s) of interest.
  • 2️⃣
    Step 2: calculate the distribution of those rows or columns in percents — the denominator is now that group's own total.
  • 3️⃣
    Step 3: graph it. Use a side-by-side bar graph or a segmented bar graph to compare distributions.
  • 🔍
    This is the only display that shows a relationship — because a relationship is a difference between conditional distributions.

Slide 14: the same two conditional distributions as a segmented (100% stacked) bar graph. Each bar is one gender, divided into the five responses.

★ Key insight

Choosing the denominator is choosing the question. "What percent of males say X" and "what percent of people who say X are male" are different numbers from the same cell, and only one of them answers the question you asked.

🎬 Conditional distributions 🎬 Side-by-side vs segmented bar graphs

🧩 Comprehension check

Among males, what percent said "some chance, but probably not", and what is the denominator?
286 / 2 459 = 11.6%. The denominator is the total number of males, because we have conditioned on being male. Using 4 826 would give 5.9% — a marginal-style number that answers a different question.
When is a segmented bar graph better than a side-by-side one?
When you want to compare the shape of whole distributions and there are several categories: each bar is forced to 100%, so differences in composition are easy to see. Side-by-side is better for comparing one specific category across groups, because the bars sit next to each other on a common baseline.
The male and female bars look similar. Does that mean there is no relationship?
It means any relationship is weak in size — but with 4 826 people even small differences can be statistically significant. This is the whole point of testing: the eye judges the size of a difference, and χ² judges whether a difference that size could plausibly be chance.

Lesson 5 of 14 · Slides 15–16, 21 · hypotheses

⚖️ Two ways of saying the same H₀

The lecture always writes the null hypothesis twice. Both wordings are examinable, and the second one is the useful one.

Slides 16 and 21. The lecture writes H₀ first as "no relation between the dependent and independent variable", then "in other words: the percentages are the same in the different groups".

  • ⚖️
    H₀: no relation between the dependent and independent variable. H₁: there is a relation.
  • 🔁
    In other words: H₀ says the percentages are the same in the different groups; H₁ says they are not.
  • 🌍
    They are claims about the population. "In the population, attending the lectures is NOT related to passing the course exam."
  • ↔️
    Chi-square hypotheses are never directional. Any departure in any direction makes χ² larger, so there is no "expected direction" to state — though the test itself uses only the right-hand tail of the χ² distribution, as Lesson 10 shows.
  • 📋
    Write both wordings in the exam. The second one is what lets you build the expected counts in Lesson 8.

📊 The same hypotheses for the lecture's example (slide 21)

Abstract wordingPercentage wording
H₀In the population, attending the lectures is NOT related to passing the course exam.In the population, the percentage of students that passed the exam is the same for the group that attended and the group that did not.
HaIn the population, attending the lectures IS related to passing the course exam.These percentages are NOT equal.
🪝 Memory hook: H₀ is the boring universe — the one where knowing which group someone is in tells you nothing new about the outcome. The test asks how strange your table would be in that universe.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "The null hypothesis says the two things have nothing to do with each other — which is the same as saying the percentages are identical in every group. The alternative just says they're not identical. Not bigger, not smaller: just not the same."
★ Key insight

"No relation" and "the same percentages in every group" are not two hypotheses — they are one hypothesis in two languages. The translation is what makes the test computable: the second version can be turned into a table of numbers, and that table is the expected counts.

🎬 Writing H₀ for a chi-square test

🧩 Comprehension check

A student writes "H₀: attending the lectures increases the chance of passing". What is wrong?
Three things. It is directional, and chi-square has no direction; it is causal, where the test is only about association; and it is stated as the alternative in the null's position. H₀ must be the no-relation claim.
Why does it matter that the hypotheses are about the population?
Because in the sample the percentages will essentially never be exactly equal. A visible difference in your table is not evidence against H₀ until you know how large a difference chance alone would routinely produce — which is exactly what the χ² distribution supplies.

Lesson 6 of 14 · Slides 17–18 · H₀ in the table

🪞 What a table with no relation looks like

The clearest two slides in the lecture: the same margins, arranged two ways.

Slides 17 and 18, side by side. Read the percentages across each row: identical rows mean no relation; different rows mean a relation.

  • 🟢
    If H₀ is true, the percentages for "no", "a little" and "a lot" are the same in the VWO group and the HAVO group — 20 / 30 / 50 in both.
  • 🔴
    If H₀ is not true, those percentages differ between the groups — 10 / 25 / 65 against 27 / 33 / 40.
  • 🧷
    The margins are identical in both tables. 100 VWO, 150 HAVO, 50 / 75 / 125 across the columns, 250 in total.
  • 🔬
    So a relation is not about the totals — it is entirely about how the counts are distributed inside them.
  • 🧮
    The H₀ table is computable. Given the margins, there is exactly one table with identical row percentages — and those are the expected counts.

🎬 What independence looks like in a table

🧩 Comprehension check

In the H₀-true table, VWO has 20 worriers and HAVO has 30. Isn't that a difference?
In counts, yes — but HAVO has 150 students against VWO's 100. As percentages both are 20%, so the groups are identical in the only sense that matters. This is precisely why the lecture converts to percentages before comparing anything.
Both tables have the same margins. What does that tell you about the degrees of freedom?
That the margins are fixed and only the inside can vary — and in a 2 × 3 table, once you choose two of the six inside cells the rest are forced. Hence df = (2 − 1)(3 − 1) = 2: exactly the number of cells you are free to choose.

🎓 Attending the lectures and passing the exam

A hundred students, two yes/no questions. This example carries the next four lessons, so learn its numbers.

Slides 20 and 22. The design: dependent variable pass (yes/no), independent variable attended (yes/no) — both qualitative, so chi-square it is.

    The question. Is the percentage of students that passed the same in the group that attended the lectures and the group that did not?
  • 🧾
    The design. Dependent variable: pass (yes/no). Independent variable: attended (yes/no). Both qualitative.

🎬 A 2 × 2 table, worked

🧩 Comprehension check

Where do 87.5% and 66.7% come from, and why not 35/75 and 40/75?
35/40 and 40/60 — each group divided by its own total, because we are conditioning on attendance. Dividing by 75 would condition on passing instead, answering "what percent of the passers attended?" — a different question with a different answer (46.7%).
The overall pass rate is 75%. Which lesson uses that number?
Lesson 8. If H₀ is true, attendance is irrelevant and everybody's chance of passing is the overall 75% — which is what turns the margins into the expected counts.

Lesson 8 of 14 · Slide 24 · expected counts

🧮 The table H₀ predicts

One formula, and the reason it is not arbitrary: it is the null hypothesis, written out as counts.

Slide 24. "Assume it doesn't matter whether you attend or not. Then the chance of passing is always 75%." The expected table is that sentence turned into counts.

  • 🧮
    The formula. expected count = row total × column total ÷ table total.
  • 💡
    Why it works. If H₀ is true the pass rate is 75% for everyone, so 75% of the 40 attenders — 30 — should pass.
  • 🔗
    Expected counts come only from the margins, never from the inside of the table. That is what makes them the H₀ prediction rather than a description of your data.
  • 🔢
    They need not be whole numbers. 34.22 bottles of wine is a perfectly good expected count — it is an average, not a person.
  • ✅
    They double as the validity check. Expected count ≥ 5 is the condition the slide flags right here.

🧪 Demo 4 · Click any cell to see its expected count

Two tables to practise on. Click a cell and the row total, column total and arithmetic are spelled out.

🪝 Memory hook: row times column over total. Or in words: "your row's share of the people, given the column's overall rate."
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "If the two variables had nothing to do with each other, each group would split the same way as everybody overall. So I take the overall rate and apply it to each group's size — that's the expected count."
★ Key insight

Expected counts are not "what I hoped to find". They are a prediction made by H₀, computed from the margins alone — which is why the test is a fair comparison: the null gets to use the same group sizes you actually observed.

🎬 Expected counts, step by step

🧩 Comprehension check

Compute the expected count for "did not attend and did not pass".
60 × 25 ÷ 100 = 15. Check: 25% of the 60 non-attenders, since the overall fail rate is 25%. The four expected counts 30, 10, 45, 15 add to 100 and reproduce both margins exactly — a useful check that you have not slipped.
Why must the expected counts add up to the same margins as the observed table?
Because they are built from those margins. Row 1's expected counts are 40 × (75/100) and 40 × (25/100), which add to 40. If your expected table does not reproduce the margins, you have made an arithmetic error.
The expected count for a cell is 3.4. What should you do?
Stop and check the validity conditions. In a 2 × 2 table all four expected counts must be at least 5, so the chi-square p-value would not be trustworthy — the usual responses are to collect more data, combine categories where that makes sense, or use an exact test.

Lesson 9 of 14 · Slides 25–26 · the statistic

➕ Adding up the surprise

Four cells, four small numbers, one total — and what the total means when it is zero.

Slide 25's calculation, cell by cell. Notice that "attend · failed" contributes the most: a gap of 5 against an expected count of only 10.

  • ➕
    The statistic. χ² = sum across all cells of (observed − expected)² ÷ expected.
  • 🔲
    Squaring removes the signs, so over- and under-counts cannot cancel — and big gaps count far more than small ones.
  • ➗
    Dividing by the expected count scales each gap to the size of the cell. A gap of 5 is trivial where 500 were expected and dramatic where 10 were.
  • 0️⃣
    χ² = 0 means observed equals expected in every cell — the table H₀ predicted, exactly. Small deviations are still likely even when H₀ is true.
  • 📈
    The logic of the test: big difference between observed and expected → large χ² → small p-value → reject H₀.

🧪 Demo 5 · Build χ² yourself

Edit the four counts of the attendance table. Expected counts, each cell's contribution, χ², df and the p-value all recompute — and the verdict changes with them.

🪝 Memory hook: each cell pays a fine for being different from what H₀ predicted — squared, and scaled by how big the cell should have been. χ² is the total fine.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "For every cell I take how far off it was, square it, and divide by what was expected there. Adding those up gives χ². Zero means the table matched the null perfectly; the bigger it gets, the worse the null looks."
★ Key insight

The division by the expected count is the part students skip and examiners test. Without it, a gap of 5 in a cell expecting 10 and a gap of 5 in a cell expecting 1 000 would count the same — and they are not remotely the same evidence.

🎬 Computing χ² by hand

🧩 Comprehension check

All four gaps are exactly 5 students. Why do the four contributions differ?
Because each gap is divided by its own expected count: 25/30 = 0.833, 25/10 = 2.500, 25/45 = 0.556, 25/15 = 1.667. The same absolute discrepancy is much more surprising in a cell that expected only 10 people.
Can χ² be negative, or exactly zero?
Never negative — every term is a square divided by a positive expected count. Exactly zero is possible in principle (observed = expected in every cell) but essentially never happens with real data, which is why the lecture notes that small deviations are still likely under H₀.
Two studies both report χ² = 12. Do they carry the same evidence?
Not necessarily — it depends on the size of the table. χ² = 12 on df = 1 is very strong evidence; on df = 20 it is entirely unremarkable, because a larger table accumulates more random contributions. That comparison is the next lesson.

📐 When is χ² too large?

🧩 Comprehension check

A 4 × 3 table gives χ² = 13.0. Significant at α = .05?
df = (4 − 1)(3 − 1) = 6. The critical value at α = .05 for df = 6 is 12.592, and 13.0 exceeds it — so yes, just barely. Note that the same χ² on df = 1 would have been overwhelming, and on df = 20 not remotely significant.
Your table is 2 × 2 and you tested 5 000 people. What is df?
1. Degrees of freedom depend only on the shape of the table, never on the sample size. What N changes is how easily a given percentage difference produces a large χ² — not the bar that χ² has to clear.
Why does the lecture say χ² "must be relatively larger" when there are many cells?
Because every cell contributes something positive, even under H₀. With 24 cells you are adding two dozen small random amounts, so the typical χ² is much bigger than in a 2 × 2 table. The critical values in the df column rise for exactly this reason.

Lesson 11 of 14 · Slides 29–30 · decision and causality

✅ p = .018 — and then the hard question

How to write the conclusion, and the three explanations that survive a significant test.

Slide 30, drawn. The lecture raises all three possibilities by name: the primary interpretation, a reversed relation, and a third or "nuisance" variable.

  • ✅
    The decision. p = .018, so H₀ has to be rejected: attending the lectures is related to the chance of passing the course exam.
  • 🗣️
    Or in percentage language: the percentage of students that passed is larger in the group that attended than in the group that did not.
  • 🎯
    Primary interpretation: attending prepares you, so your chance of passing rises.
  • 🔄
    Reversed relation? Here it is implausible — the exam takes place after the lectures, so passing cannot have caused attendance.
  • 🧩
    Third variable? Motivation would produce this table on its own: motivated students attend, and motivated students pass.

🧪 Demo 7 · Name the alternative explanation

Six significant chi-square results. For each, decide which alternative explanation is the serious one — reversed direction, a third variable, or neither (a design that actually supports the causal reading).

📊 How to write the conclusion (and how not to)

Write thisNot this
"χ²(1) = 5.56, p = .018. Attending the lectures is related to passing the exam.""Attending the lectures makes you pass." — a causal claim the design cannot support.
"The percentage passing was higher among attenders (87.5%) than non-attenders (66.7%).""The groups were significantly different." — different in what? Always name the direction and the percentages.
"H₀ is rejected at α = .05.""H₀ is false." / "H₁ is proven." — a test never proves anything, it rejects or fails to reject.
"A third variable such as motivation could produce this pattern."Silence about alternatives, when the study is observational.
🪝 Memory hook: χ² is a smoke detector. It tells you reliably that something is burning; it has no opinion whatsoever about what lit the fire.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "The p-value was .018, so the difference is bigger than chance would usually give — attendance and passing really are related. But students chose whether to attend, so motivation could be behind both, and the test can't separate those."
★ Key insight

A significant χ² licenses exactly one sentence: the percentages differ in the population. Every further word — causes, helps, leads to — is an argument from the design, not from the test.

🎬 Third variables in observational data 🎬 Reporting χ² in APA style

🧩 Comprehension check

Why does the lecture dismiss the reversed explanation so quickly here?
Because of timing: the lectures happen before the exam, so passing cannot have caused attendance. Time order is the cheapest way to rule out a reversed arrow — and it is not always available, which is why the alternative has to be considered rather than assumed away.
How would you design the study so the causal reading is defensible?
Assign attendance rather than letting students choose — for example, randomly giving half the cohort access to a session. Random assignment makes motivation equal on average across the groups, so it can no longer explain a difference in pass rates.
If p had been .18 instead of .018, what would you write?
"χ²(1) = 1.8, p = .18: we fail to reject H₀; there is not enough evidence that attendance is related to passing in the population." Not "there is no relation" — failing to find evidence is not evidence of absence, especially with only 100 students.

🍷 Wine, music and a 3 × 3 table

The same four steps on a bigger table — and the first time you have to find the p-value between two table values.

🎬 A 3 × 3 chi-square, worked 🎬 Reading cell contributions

🧩 Comprehension check

Verify the expected count for Italian wine under French music.
Row total 31 × column total 75 ÷ 243 = 2 325 ÷ 243 = 9.57. Only one bottle was actually sold, so the contribution is (1 − 9.57)² ÷ 9.57 = 73.4 ÷ 9.57 = 7.67 — the single largest term in the statistic.
Why is the p-value reported as an interval rather than a number?
Because a printed chi-square table only lists selected probabilities. 18.28 falls between the df = 4 entries for .0025 (16.42) and .001 (18.47), so all the table can say is that p lies between them. Software would report p = .0011 exactly.
The store manager asks "so which music should I play?" What do you say?
That the test only establishes the distributions differ, and the cell contributions say the effect is on matching national wines — French music raised the French share from 36% to 52%, and Italian music raised Italian wine from 13% to 23%. Whether that raises revenue depends on margins, and on whether a single store on particular days generalises at all.

Lesson 13 of 14 · Slides 37–38 · large tables and validity

🧑‍🏫 Big tables, and when the test is allowed

Seven tutors, 240 students — and the three conditions that decide whether your p-value means anything.

Slide 37's SPSS crosstab, drawn as percentages. The differences look striking — 82.5% against 55.0% — but with df = 6 and only 20 students in the smallest groups, χ² = 6.67 gives p = .35: not significant.

  • 🧮
    Large tables work the same way. A 2 × 7 table of pass/fail by tutor gives df = (2 − 1)(7 − 1) = 6.
  • ⚖️
    More cells, bigger χ² by chance — so with large df, χ² must be relatively larger before it is significant.
  • 👀
    Percentages can look dramatic and still not be significant. Tutor 7's 55% comes from 11 students out of 20.
  • 🎲
    Two valid sampling designs: independent SRSs from two or more populations, each individual classified on one categorical variable; or a single SRS with each individual classified on both variables.
  • ✅
    Three conditions: the average expected count at least 5; all individual cell counts at least 1; and in a 2 × 2 table, all four expected counts at least 5.

★ Key insight

Conditions ① and ③ are about expected counts, and ② is normally read the same way (Moore & McCabe: every individual expected count at least 1), so an observed zero is not in itself a problem. Note that ① asks only for the average expected count to reach 5 — a single small expected count is tolerated in a large table, and only in a 2 × 2 table must all four reach 5.

🎬 The expected-count conditions 🎬 What to do when the counts are too small

🧩 Comprehension check

Tutor 7's group passed at 55% against an overall 75%. Why isn't that significant?
Because it is 11 students out of 20. With samples that small, a run of four extra failures is unremarkable — and the test has to judge all seven groups at once, on df = 6, which raises the bar to 12.592. The total χ² is only 6.67.
A 4 × 5 table has twenty cells; three of them have expected counts of 3. Is the test valid?
Possibly. The rule for larger tables is that the average expected count is at least 5 and every cell count is at least 1 — individual expected counts below 5 are tolerated in a big table. Check the average across all twenty cells before deciding; if it fails, combine categories that belong together.
Which condition applies to your 2 × 2 attendance table, and does it pass?
The strict one: all four expected counts at least 5. They are 30, 10, 45 and 15 — comfortably clear, so the test is valid.

Lesson 14 of 14 · Slides 39–49 · goodness of fit

🍫 One variable against a claim

The second flavour of chi-square: same formula, expected counts from a claim instead of from the margins.

Slides 42 and 45: a bag of 60 M&M's, observed against what Mars's claimed colour distribution predicts. The two visible gaps are yellow (15 against 8.4) and blue (9 against 14.4).

  • 1️⃣
    One variable, one row of counts. A one-way table displays the distribution of a categorical variable for the individuals in a sample.
  • 📜
    H₀ is a list of proportions: pblue = 0.24, porange = 0.20, pgreen = 0.16, pyellow = 0.14, pred = 0.13, pbrown = 0.13. Ha: at least one proportion differs from the claim.
  • ✖️
    Expected count = claimed proportion × sample size. Blue: 0.24 × 60 = 14.40.
  • ➕
    Same statistic: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E = 2.025 + 1.333 + 0.600 + 5.186 + 0.621 + 0.415 = 10.180.
  • 📐
    df = k − 1 = 5, giving p between .05 and .10 — so we fail to reject H₀ and do not conclude the company's claim is wrong.

The lecture asks this directly. You could test "24% of all M&M's are blue" with a one-sample z test for a proportion — but doing that for every colour is inefficient and creates multiple comparisons, and it would not tell you how likely it is to get a bag of 60 whose whole colour distribution differs this much from the claim, taking all the colours into account at once. 📊 The lecture's other goodness-of-fit example (slide 40)

Flower colours, with 1 000 plants: observed 160 red, 640 pink, 200 white against an expected 250 / 500 / 250 — a 1 : 2 : 1 genetic ratio. Here the expected counts come from a theory rather than a company's claim, but the machinery is identical: χ² = 90²/250 + 140²/500 + 50²/250 = 32.4 + 39.2 + 10.0 = 81.6 on df = 3 − 1 = 2, far beyond the .001 critical value of 13.815. 🪝 Memory hook: independence builds its expectations from the table; goodness of fit builds them from a promise someone made. Everything after that is the same arithmetic.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Someone claims the percentages should be 24, 20, 16, 14, 13, 13. I multiply each by my sample size to get what I'd expect, compare with what I counted, and add up the (O − E)²/E terms. With six categories that's 5 degrees of freedom."
★ Key insight

df = k − 1, not k, for the same reason as always: once you know five of the six counts and the total of 60, the sixth is forced. Degrees of freedom count the numbers you are free to choose, and that is one fewer than the number of categories.

🎬 Goodness of fit, worked 🎬 Why not many separate tests

🧩 Comprehension check

A bag of 200 M&M's, same claimed percentages. What are the expected counts?
Multiply each claimed proportion by 200: blue 48, orange 40, green 32, yellow 28, red 26, brown 26. df is still 5 — degrees of freedom depend on the number of categories, never on the sample size. But the same percentage discrepancy in a bag of 200 produces a much larger χ², which is why bigger samples detect smaller departures.
The lecture fails to reject H₀. Does that mean Mars's claim is correct?
No. It means one bag of 60 sweets is not enough evidence against it. The yellow cell alone contributed 5.19 of the 10.18, and with a larger sample that same imbalance would be significant. "Fail to reject" is a statement about the evidence, never a proof of H₀.
How do you know whether an exam question wants independence or goodness of fit?
Count the variables. Two categorical variables crossed in a table, with the expected counts built from its margins, is independence. One categorical variable with a claimed set of proportions supplied in the question is goodness of fit — and the giveaway is that the question hands you percentages from outside the data.

🧵 Where this leaves you

★ The thesis of the whole lecture

Chi-square is one move, performed three times: build the table H₀ predicts, then measure how far the real table is from it. The expected counts are the null hypothesis written out as counts — which is why row × column ÷ total is exactly "the same percentages in every group". Each cell's (observed − expected)² / expected is how surprising that cell is, scaled by how much surprise its size can afford, and χ² is the total. Whether the total is large cannot be judged by eye, because a bigger table collects more of it by accident — hence degrees of freedom, which raise the bar as the table grows. The goodness-of-fit test changes only where the expectations come from: a claim about proportions instead of the table's own margins. And a significant result says the percentages differ, not why, because a third variable outside the table can produce exactly the same counts. So: percentages to see it, expected counts to test it, degrees of freedom to judge it, and judgement to explain it.
